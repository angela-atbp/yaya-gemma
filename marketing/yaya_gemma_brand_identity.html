01 — Strategic Foundation
The Emotional Brief

Before any color or font is chosen, we must understand what our user feels in the moment she reaches for this app — and what she needs to feel after.

User arrives feeling…
😓 Overwhelmed — "What do I even make tonight?"
😔 Guilty — "I wasted those carrots again"
😤 Frustrated — "I just spent €80 at the market"
🌀 Decision-fatigued — too many options, none feel right
⏱ Time-poor — she needs an answer, not an experience
→
Yaya Gemma makes her feel…
✅ Capable — "I can actually do this"
🌿 Relieved — the guilt dissolves into action
🧠 Smart — "I used everything, wasted nothing"
🤝 Supported — like a warm, wise presence in the kitchen
🏡 At home — not like she's using tech, but being helped
02 — Brand Archetype
The Wise Nurturer

Yaya Gemma sits at the intersection of two archetypes: The Caregiver (warmth, support, no judgment) and The Sage (resourceful, quietly brilliant). This is the grandmother who never wastes a thing — and never makes you feel bad about it.

ARCHETYPE — PRIMARY
The
Caregiver

Protective, warm, generous. She feeds people. She solves the 6pm panic without drama. She never says "you should have planned better." She just says: here's dinner.

ARCHETYPE — SECONDARY
The
Sage

Quietly resourceful. Knows what the half-wilted courgette can become. Never shows off. The wisdom is in the result — a good meal from what you already had.

THE BRAND IN ONE SENTENCE

"Technology with a grandmother's heart."

Not a recipe database. Not a meal-planner. A presence — warm and brilliant — that starts where you are, with what you have.

WHAT COMPETITORS FEEL LIKE

Efficient strangers.
Yaya feels like family.

Tasty, Yummly, Allrecipes — they send you to the store. Yaya opens your fridge and says: "I see dinner."

Why This Palette Works for Your User

Research shows overwhelmed users respond poorly to high-contrast tech-blue palettes — they increase cortisol (stress). Warm earth tones (terracotta, sage, cream) signal safety, home, and competence without the sterility of a clinical UI. The butter gold is reserved exclusively for win states — so every time the user sees it, it means: you did something good.

04 — Typography
Old Soul, Modern Voice

A deliberate serif/sans pairing that balances warmth with clarity. The serif carries Yaya's wisdom. The grotesque carries her practicality. Neither competes.

Display — Headlines — Taglines
Cormorant
Garamond

Dinner sorted. Nothing wasted.

Weights: 400 Regular, 600 SemiBold, 700 Bold + Italic. Used for hero text, section headers, emotional statements, taglines. The italic adds warmth — use it for the brand name and key phrases.
UI — Body — Labels — Navigation
Bricolage
Grotesque

Cook with what you have. Love what you make.

Weights: 300 Light, 400 Regular, 500 Medium, 600 SemiBold. Used for body copy, buttons, navigation labels, UI text, captions. Approachable geometry — feels warm, not corporate.

"What's in your fridge? That's tonight's dinner."

Taglines and hero moments use Cormorant italic at scale. The editorial quality signals this isn't another generic app — it has a point of view. Below the hero, Bricolage Grotesque takes over: clear, efficient, warm.

05 — Brand Personality
Six Traits. Every Touchpoint.

Every word, color, and interaction should feel like one of these traits. If it doesn't — cut it.

🫂
Supportive
Never lectures. Never says "you should have planned." Suggests. Enables. Celebrates the small win of using the last of the onions.
NOT: preachy, moralistic, guilt-inducing
🔍
Resourceful
Sees possibility where others see a bare fridge. Every half-used ingredient is a starting point. Waste reduction is a side effect of good cooking — not a lecture topic.
NOT: wasteful aesthetics, buying-forward messaging
🌡
Warm
Real warmth — a grandmother, not a chatbot pretending to have feelings. Soft visual language. Gentle copy. Never urgent for the sake of urgency.
NOT: cold, minimal-for-status, tech-bro detached
🪨
Honest
No manufactured FOMO. No fake countdown timers. No dark patterns. The app earns trust by telling the truth — including when an ingredient genuinely can't substitute.
NOT: gamified guilt, fake urgency, clickbait
🌿
Sustainable
Zero waste is a natural outcome, not a performance. Never preach it. Let the rescued spinach speak for itself. ESG angle is for B2B; users just want good dinner.
NOT: greenwashing, virtue signalling, hectoring
⚡
Decisive
The user is overwhelmed. Yaya is not. She cuts through the decision fatigue with a clear answer: here is what you cook tonight, here is how. No endless scroll of options.
NOT: feature-dumping, choice overload, indecisive
06 — Imagery Direction
Real Kitchens. Real Hands. Real Food.

No food-porn perfection. No pristine marble countertops with a single sprig of thyme. Yaya's world looks like your kitchen — lived-in, warm, and real.

✅ Use This

  • Overhead shots of actual fridge shelves — imperfect, real
  • Hands cutting, stirring, plating — motion, life
  • Worn wooden chopping boards, ceramic bowls
  • Warm evening kitchen light — golden hour indoors
  • Steam, condensation, natural imperfection
  • Food in various states: raw, cooking, finished
  • A rescued bunch of wilting herbs becoming something beautiful

❌ Avoid This

  • Perfect marble countertops & spotless kitchens
  • AI-generated food that looks too perfect
  • Styled hero shots with single garnishes
  • Cold white studio lighting
  • Unrealistic produce (no wilting = not our world)
  • Stock photo families with identical smiles
  • Thin-plate fine dining aesthetics

The "Rescue Moment"

The single most ownable visual in the Yaya universe: a slightly-wilted vegetable, or a near-empty fridge, transformed into a plated meal. This before/after is your hero shot. It is emotionally true, it is the product promise, and it stops the scroll.

07 — App Store Assets
What Goes Where

App store pages are your brand's most-seen real estate. Every frame is a micro-pitch to someone who has 8 seconds of attention.

App Icon Direction

The icon must read at 60×60px and communicate "food + intelligence + warmth" without a single word.

Recommended concept: A stylised open pot or bowl shape — viewed from a slight angle — on a deep terracotta background. The steam forms a subtle leaf or spark shape. No text. No chef's hat (too generic).

Background: Terracotta #C4572A

Foreground: Linen white #F5EFE6

App Name & Subtitle

App stores allow 30 chars for name, 30 for subtitle. Use every character.

App Name (30 chars)
Yaya Gemma
Subtitle (iOS) / Short description (Android)
Cook what you have. Waste nothing.

09 — Brand Rules
Do & Don't

These are non-negotiable. Any asset — screenshot, social post, email header — that violates a "don't" breaks the brand.

✓ Always Do
Lead with the user's problem, not the product feature
Use Cormorant italic for emotional hero moments
Let terracotta signal action; sage signal trust
Show real, imperfect food and real kitchens
Reserve golden butter exclusively for win/success states
Keep copy short — Yaya speaks in sentences, not paragraphs
Use "you" and "your fridge" — make it personal
Celebrate rescue moments visually and verbally
✗ Never Do
Use green gradients on white — it looks like every other food app
Use Inter, Roboto, or system fonts for display text
Show perfect food-porn imagery — it alienates the overwhelmed user
Use sustainability/zero-waste as the headline — it's a side effect, not the pitch
Use red for anything except genuine danger/expiry states
Write more than 2 sentences of body copy before the first CTA
Use the word "AI" in hero copy — users want dinner, not a tech pitch
Compete with the user's attention — remove everything non-essential
10 — Voice Quick-Reference
How Yaya Speaks

For app store copy, push notifications, and screenshot captions — the voice must be consistent across EN and FR.

Warm · Direct · No Waffle
✓ Write this

"Open your fridge. We'll handle dinner."

"That spinach has 2 days left. Here's what to make."

Cold · Corporate · Vague
✗ Avoid this

"Leverage AI-powered culinary intelligence to optimise your pantry utilisation."

"Our innovative solution helps reduce food waste through machine learning."

The Yaya Test
Would Yaya say it?

Before publishing any copy, ask: would a warm, wise grandmother — who happens to know exactly what's in your fridge — say this? If it sounds like a startup pitch deck, rewrite it.
